# Concepts

## SM-001 — The Personal Iceberg Metaphor has eight levels

- Evidence: `[official claim]`
- Source: `satir-model-1991#fig-iceberg`, `banmen-satir-yesterday-today-2002#fig1`
- Confidence: `source-faithful`

Behaviour sits above the waterline. Beneath it, in order: coping stance, feelings, feelings about those feelings, perceptions, expectations, yearnings, and Self. The first six are commonly grouped as doing; yearnings and Self as being.

Two qualifications matter. The iceberg was co-created by John Banmen from observing Satir's work, with Jane Gerber and Maria Gomori. It is not a diagram Satir herself published. And Banmen's own running prose gives a shorter five-item list; the eight-level form lives in his figure. Current institute writing sometimes inserts body sensations and splits out core beliefs. The eight-level list is standard, but the exact wording is not policed.

## SM-002 — Four survival stances, with congruence as the goal

- Evidence: `[official claim]`
- Source: `banmen-satir-yesterday-today-2002#p17-18`
- Confidence: `source-faithful`

Placating, blaming, super-reasonable, and irrelevant. Congruence is the fifth, healthy response and the goal state rather than a survival stance; levelling is Satir's earlier word for it.

Banmen is explicit that these are ways of surviving under emotional stress and not personality categories. Any use of them as a type indicator contradicts the model's own literature. A five-stance list adding withdrawing circulates in coaching material; it is a practitioner variant, not canon.

## SM-003 — Each stance has a preferred entry point

- Evidence: `[official claim]`
- Source: `banmen-satir-yesterday-today-2002#entry`
- Confidence: `source-faithful`

Reach a placating person through their feelings. Reach a blaming person through their expectations, meaning what they want rather than how they feel. Reach a super-reasonable person through their perceptions first, then body reactions, then expectations, and only then feelings. An irrelevant or distracting person is hardest to reach and is approached through body sensations, physical activity, and the immediate context.

This is the most operationally useful piece of the model for a coaching conversation.

## SM-004 — Ingredients of an Interaction

- Evidence: `[official claim]`
- Source: `satir-model-1991#p121ff`
- Confidence: `mixed`

A sequence for taking apart one exchange: what was seen and heard, the meaning made of it, the feeling about that meaning, the feeling about that feeling, the defence used, the rule about what may be said, and the response. Step counts of seven, five, and four all circulate. The seventh step is inferred rather than read directly.

## SM-005 — The Change Process Model is canonical but its stage list is not

- Evidence: `[inference]`
- Source: `satir-model-1991#ch5`
- Confidence: `tentative`

The 1991 book has a chapter on the process of change, so the model is genuinely part of the canon. But no Satir body publishes a staged version. Banmen's 2002 paper contains no mention of a foreign element, chaos, or a transforming idea at all.

At least seven mutually inconsistent renderings circulate with four, five, or six stages. The closest to an authoritative rendering is Jean McLendon's 1999 summary diagram: old status quo, foreign element, chaos, transforming ideas, integration, practice, new status quo.

**Treat any specific stage list as attributable to its renderer, never to Satir.** See `limitations.md` on the software version.
